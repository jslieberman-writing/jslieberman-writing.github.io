Why Hasn’t SPO Taken Off with Politics and Public Affairs (Yet)?

Jordan Lieberman

Jul 17, 2025
05/01/2023

The 2024 election will break advertising and fundraising records, with the Presidential election, every seat in the House and a razor-thin Senate majority on the line.

Ad Impact, a political research company, forecasts 2024 US political cycle spend to cross the $10 billion mark for the first time ever.

But with that kind of money pouring in, and with online ads as the fastest-growing media, one has to ask, why do so few political and public affairs advertisers embrace supply-path optimization (SPO)?

For one thing, DSPs have done a better job at navigating political agencies and advertisers, compared to the publisher side. Large DSPs hired evangelists and had more staying power in DC advertising circles. Second, political ad buyers are mostly unaware of the added friction when a DSP is added to the equation, rather than going directly to publishers and SSPs.

Ultimately, political advertisers rarely choose to develop publisher-direct relationships, because it’s a heavy lift (DSPs aggregate those deals) and political agencies tend to have leaner digital teams in the off years. Publisher direct deals are relegated to the back burner.

Missing the SPO opportunity

There are approximately two hundred experienced professionals who decide the vast majority of political ad spending. And that narrows down even further for digital advertising. Which means there is a clear opportunity to educate political advertisers and shift them to SPO as a strategy.

The most clear-cut reason for political advertisers to invest in SPO is efficiency. If Pepsi reaches a Coca-Cola drinker, or was targeting women for a particular campaign but ads served to men, those impressions still hold some value. Political ads served to people who don’t live in the right state or district—or that reach an opponent’s supporter—are truly wasted.

Political advertisers must also sharpen their practice because they’re popular targets of ad fraudsters. A report by the University of Baltimore estimated $377 million of political digital advertising went to fraud in 2020, and was likely far higher in the 2022 midterms. The urgency of late-cycle spending creates less accountability.

Political strategists and ad buyers have improved KPIs and reach-and-frequency discipline in recent cycles. But even with these gains, political advertising still hasn’t embraced SPO. Not yet.

A path for SPO

Political advertisers have missed out on SPO due to education gaps and structural inertia. Most agencies with in-house buying are unaware of how much value is lost to middlemen.

The DSP dilemma is especially acute in swing states, where opposing campaigns and aligned super PACs bid against each other for identical inventory. Rates rise, platforms win, and neither advertisers nor publishers benefit.

SPO allows campaigns to serve fewer impressions while still hitting reach and frequency goals in specific states or districts.

Political advertisers are beginning to evaluate SPO products. In prior cycles, most defaulted to one or two DSPs. Today, consultants are questioning whether to swim upstream and reconnect advertisers with publishers.

This shift is accelerated by platform retreat. Meta, Microsoft, Yahoo, Verizon, and others have downsized or exited political sales. That leaves room for political-native technologists to prove new models.

SPO is a practical way to clean up the political media supply chain. We can’t control election outcomes—but we can make the advertising system work better for everyone involved.

Jordan Lieberman is the CEO of Powers Interactive.
