5 Ways Campaigns Were Misled in 2016

Jordan Lieberman

Jul 17, 2025
11/25/2016

I have news for you. Many of you were misled in 2016. You bought questionable digital inventory to get a slightly cheaper rate, used probabilistic data but thought you were getting deterministic, reached a tiny fraction of your target universe, and got some really bad advice.

Unfortunately, this was all too common. This cycle saw an unusually high number of new entrants into the consulting market, particularly on digital. Knowing there was a flood of newcomers with irrationally large sales goals, it’s easier to understand why our credibility is threatened. My view is that the best thing we can do as an industry is call BS.

Questionable Inventory

The cheapest inventory providers aren't giving you a better deal. They’re just selling skirt steak and calling it Kobe strip. You can have affordable inventory, high-viewability inventory, and high-reach inventory — but not all at the same time. The best vendors will help you prioritize two of those. The worst will promise all three.

Because the vast majority of political and public affairs buying in 2016 was aimed at persuasion, the priority was typically maximizing reach. It’s virtually impossible to rely solely on premium, private marketplace deals with a narrow target set and timeframe. You should be cautious when publishers suggest otherwise. I’ve even seen first-party data supplemented with lookalikes just to meet delivery goals.

Lower Quality Data and Fuzzy Targeting

We utilize tens of thousands of data points, mainly based on commercial behavior and demographics. Modeled data is, by definition, less precise. So when a vendor offers pre-constructed data on an insanely narrow issue, they may be fibbing.

National surveys scaled down to congressional districts or zip codes are much cruder than vendors imply. It’s an attractive pitch, and I understand why less informed clients are excited. In 2016, I lost business to a shop offering a file of “people interested in infrastructure.” That’s absurd. As an alternative, we suggested targeting known car owners with long commutes. It wasn’t as sexy.

Worse practices exist. In 2015, we caught a consulting firm pretending to offer Audience Partners targeting when they weren’t. Their clients were likely buying cheap, non-targeted inventory. It was the first case I saw of possibly counterfeit voter-targeted digital ads — and it won’t be the last.

Buyers also misunderstood IP targeting. Only carrier-provided IP data is accurate at the household level. Many of you bought IP-targeted ads without carrier data and, if lucky, hit the right zip code.

Smaller Reach with Any Single Tactic

Pick one tactic plus narrow targeting and you’ve significantly reduced your reach. Mobile-only solutions are no better than display-only solutions. Twitter’s reach among active users is under 17 percent and skews young and unpersuadable.

The median likely voter in America is 59. Meanwhile, 93 percent of Snapchat users are under 55. Video-only strategies over short timeframes also suffer when layered with small segments. Even Facebook-only advertising limits reach. “All of the above” strategies almost always perform best.

Any single DSP will also have reach problems. Multiple DSPs aggregate inventory, increase reach, and drive higher frequency. Just because you’re targeting all likely independents doesn’t mean they’re on one platform.

Ugly Programmatic TV

Programmatic television in 2016 was terrible. You could choose reach, quality, or affordability — but only two. eMarketer estimated total programmatic TV spend at about $700 million. That’s nothing.

It moved slowly due to technical hurdles, network hesitation, and fragmented viewing behavior. Spending would double, then double again, before several boom-and-bust cycles finally forced supply and demand to converge. The solutions pitched in 2016 were destined to be replaced.

Funky Advice

Pile on low-reach formats, junk data, and inexperienced media planning and you get crap on crap on crap. Roughly 95 percent of political and public affairs media flows through consultants. The best ones ask questions.

Don’t be afraid to learn how targeted ads are delivered and challenge conventional wisdom.

The takeaway from 2016: If it’s too good to be true, it probably is. Those who play the long game build trust. And sometimes it’s good to say no — even to people you trust — to avoid mistakes.

Jordan Lieberman is the CEO of Powers Interactive, a programmatic media company.
