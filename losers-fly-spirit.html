Losers Fly Spirit

Jordan Lieberman

Nov 18, 2025

What 20 years of campaign spending reveals about winners and losers

The first campaign lesson I learned was in 1998, from the best campaign manager in the business, Kent Gates: time is your only nonrenewable resource. What follows is how different kinds of operations spend it, waste it, and sometimes learn to buy it back. Kent does not allow his staff to fly on Spirit, because Spirit is for losers.

Election spend archives aren’t just a record of spending; they are part of a permanent record that we get to judge later. They reveal that when campaigns try to save money on logistics, they inevitably end up spending their most critical resource: time. Here is the evidence, written in airline codes, motel chains, and fast-food receipts.

A note about the crappiness of this data, something I’ve talked about for awhile: reading just my data, one would wonder why federal candidates showing Facebook buys in their reports win just 44% of their campaigns. Wix, 35%. Quickbooks, 21%? Seems low. But here’s the trick — better organized campaigns can afford to hire Powers Interactive or any of my less capable competitors. The DIY crowd loses most times.

Air Travel: Winners Fly Direct

If money tells you who a campaign values, flights tell you what it thinks time is worth. Every airline line in this ledger is a vote for how much lateness the staff can tolerate. Some operations buy redundancy and flexibility. Others buy the cheapest ticket they can and hope the weather behaves. Hope, it turns out, has a bad on-time record.

Air travel is one of the cleanest indicators of campaign maturity. The file shows that when teams pay for reliability, win rate lands north of 50%. When they chase bargains, it drops into the thirties.

  • Virgin Air, 65% win rate
  • American Airlines, 56% win rate
  • Southwest, 54% win rate
  • United Air, 52% win rate
  • JetBlue, 52% win rate
  • Frontier Airlines, 45% win rate
  • Spirit Airlines, 35% win rate

Campaigns that fly these carriers tend to operate like grown-ups: travel booked early, schedules tight, itineraries confirmed, focused on anything else. The win rate, roughly 55–57%, reads like competence expressed as a number.

Then there’s Spirit, at 35%. It smells like sadness and last-minute desperation. The 20-point gap between premium and budget carriers isn’t about comfort. It’s about schedule reliability and recovery options when plans collapse.

How to Keep Your Dignity and Get Clean Sheets

I have stayed in some of the gnarliest motels in America, and can assure you the dozens of dollars we saved ended up affecting zero elections. You can read a campaign’s self-respect in its lodging.

Mainstream hotels with a DC presence dominate the top of the win-rate list: Hilton, Hyatt, Hotel George, St. Regis. Hilton shows a slight edge due to proximity to incumbents. Marriott wins on footprint. Comfort Inn wins on nothing.

Then there is the campaign equivalent of Dollar General. Ramada, Motel 6, Days Inn, Red Roof Inn, Clarion, Comfort Inn — win rates below 45%, the scent of mildew and future loss drifting in at sunrise. These are emergency decisions, not strategy.

The Dark Side of Office Supplies

Account-based vendors win 62%. Retail counter vendors win 38%. The starkest divide is Staples versus Staples Advantage. One screams planning and credit checks; the other is a Friday-night panic run praying the doors don’t close at 8 p.m.

Campaigns pushing more than 15% of operating dollars through retail vendors in October usually lose. Those that lock in account relationships early and keep October under 10% win nearly two-thirds of the time.

Power Eats Protein; Losers Eat Bojangles

Across twenty years, committees dropped tens of millions at Capital Grille, the Monocle, Ruth’s Chris, Morton’s, Charlie Palmer, Smith & Wollensky. Committees spending meaningfully here run 63–71% win rates.

Fast food tells the opposite story.

  • Panera, 50% win rate
  • Domino’s, 43% win rate
  • Dunkin, 34% win rate
  • Chick-fil-A, 73% win rate
  • Bojangles / Zaxby’s, 45–53%

Chick-fil-A breaks the rule. It is operational excellence disguised as fast food.

Sometimes Authenticity > Competence

Every rule has its outlier. In rural Colorado, improvisation is the infrastructure. What looks like inefficiency in the ledger can be intimacy in practice.

Make Good Decisions

Winning vendors are predictable, scalable, and recoverable when something goes wrong. Losing vendors reflect late choices, retail dependence, and the illusion of thrift.

Time is your only nonrenewable resource. The campaigns that win don’t just spend more. They plan better.
