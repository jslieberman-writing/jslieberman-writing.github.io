The AI Revolution is Canceled

Originally published on Substack.

Seventeen years ago, C&E published a magazine cover story with the ominous headline: “Artificial Intelligence Could Alter Campaigning As We Know It.” The piece focused on how pollsters would be better able to measure sentiment, and how the lack of a natural language processing model and seven-figure costs were holding back a transformative change in the way campaigns are conducted.

From the perspective of 2023, it was wildly inaccurate. (Full disclosure: at the time, I was the publisher.)

Deep fakes weren’t on anyone’s radar then. Absolutely nobody foresaw how AI could facilitate the mass production of facts and images that consultants could deliver to targeted audiences. Stephen Colbert had just coined “truthiness” — where if it’s believable, it’s true.

Here we are nearly two decades later, and now we know: AI is really good at making any information look true. Not only that, but costs are now zero, and the only way you know a natural language processor didn’t write this is because, well, I said so.

And yet despite all that, AI hasn’t taken over campaigns. There will be no tidy, overnight AI revolution in politics and public affairs. If that’s a letdown, let me remind you there are no flying cars or colonies on Mars either.

AI is a long way from replacing general consultants. Instead, it’s creeping in to fill inefficiencies in creative production and copywriting. Think of it like your first microwave — not Rosie from the Jetsons.

The 1990s version of this was introducing a Pitney Bowes postage machine so volunteers didn’t have to lick thousands of stamps. The 2023 version is tools like ChatGPT and Quiller.

Deep fakes are an insidious sideshow, but they don’t define AI. What’s next isn’t replacement — it’s augmentation.

The McKinsey Global Institute estimates AI could automate 29.5 percent of hours worked. That number is likely lower for politics and public affairs. The smarter play is mastering today’s tools, not planning staff cuts.

In 2006 we asked whether AI would replace the general consultant, like the computer WOPR in WarGames. That idea is just as fantastical now as it was then.

Politics, like war, is an art. Computers won’t run campaigns until the singularity arrives — if it ever does.

The AI revolution predicted two decades ago is canceled. What replaced it are quiet conveniences: better targeting, faster copy, automated analysis, and optimization behind the scenes.

Replacing the general consultant? Nope.

Jordan Lieberman is the CEO of Powers Interactive, a programmatic media company.
