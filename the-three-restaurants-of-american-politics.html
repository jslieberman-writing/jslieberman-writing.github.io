The Three Restaurants of American Politics

Jordan Lieberman

Apr 21, 2026

Today I will take you on a journey through the three restaurants of politics like the American political consulting version of David Attenborough.

The Federal Election Commission has, entirely by accident, produced the most comprehensive study of feeding behavior in the history of American political science. Over two decades, more than nine thousand unique restaurant entities appear in the campaign finance record, covering about 350,000 transactions.

I tracked them. Obsessively categorized them. Ate at the good ones. And the bad ones. Watched them rise and fall, with their final transactions frozen in the FEC database like insects in amber, associated with committees that no longer exist.

What emerges is a caste system. Perfectly preserved. Remarkably stable. Almost entirely unconscious to the species that operates within it. In the end, you eat where you belong.

Level One: These are Not Restaurants

Domino’s. Pizza Hut. Chick-fil-A. Dunkin. The infrastructure of managed nutritional decline.

At the base of the ecosystem, we find about 293k people earning dozens of dollars or less. They need to eat. These are not restaurants in any meaningful sense. They are caloric delivery systems optimized for campaigns. Most people I knew on the campaign trail couldn’t maintain weight; either forgetting to eat or stress eating. It’s one or the other.

Domino’s touches more campaigns than any other restaurant in the dataset. Their app was designed for someone with too much responsibility, no time, and a digestive system that gave up complaining. I have absolutely ordered a cheese pizza on their app with my thumbs while driving on a bridge. It’s that easy.

In competitive ecosystems, the organism that removes friction wins. Domino’s does not win because it is good. In fact, it’s disgusting. It wins because in 2011 it built an app that requires the least thought.

Level Two: Performance as Food Service

Higher in the canopy, behavior changes entirely. They are performance spaces. The meal is incidental, sometimes nonexistent. What is being purchased is proximity to the right tables, the right rooms, the right people who stand when the correct person walks in. The most grizzled eaters always sit facing the door.

There are numerous performances in the animal kingdom that demonstrate one’s role in social hierarchy. We are no different. The hierarchy is communicated entirely through how crisply you stand (if at all) when a VIP exits the stairwell and turns right inside the Capitol Hill Club.

I was 19 the first time I went to the Monocle. I spent most of the meal trying to act like I belonged while Strom Thurmond sat silently, trying to eat soup. Level-crossing is rare. If given the opportunity, take it.

A Brief Interlude: In Memoriam

Bistro Bis served 860 committees and absorbed over $5.5m in political meals. It was, by any reasonable measure, infrastructure with French onion soup. Congressional staff ate there. Lobbyists maintained tables. Relationships of every flavor were managed there for years. It is gone now.

Charlie Palmer Steak. 715 transactions. 343 committees. Also gone. The loss of the steak frisée salad is a real tragedy for political professionals. Paired with three gin and tonics for lunch, it made dealing with the former owner of Campaigns & Elections tolerable.

Hard Times Cafe. Gone. I took my eldest to the Arlington Hard Times as a baby. All four chili options fed Leadership Institute students long before Cheesecake Factory arrived next door and ruined the neighborhood.

The Caucus Room. Gone. I never cared for it. It felt like an Aaron Sorkin prop.

Johnny’s Half Shell. Positioned perfectly between the Senate and Union Station, it was geographically and gastronomically accessible. Unlike most political spending, its business actually shifted down during campaign season, which computes. Business was politically balanced but leaned very slightly GOP.

Signatures, a restaurant owned by Jack Abramoff, operated less like a business and more like an extension of a lobbying apparatus. Meals were comped, billed to clients, or quietly absorbed into a larger system of influence. Over the course of less than two years, it gave away hundreds of thousands of dollars in food and drink. They always made me pay full price.

Level Three: Senior Enlisted Food

CAVA. Jason’s Deli. Wawa, feeding the most competent people in the building.

This tier feeds the people who actually prevent campaigns from collapsing. They are not glamorous. My personal requirements in this tier were always the same: free parking, free refills, and free Wi-Fi. Bonus if you do not actively hate yourself after eating there.

The meals are fast, predictable, and precisely calibrated to feel professional without requiring pretense, a mirror of the people they are intended to serve. CAVA spans 332 committees and $400k+ in documented transactions. It does not aspire to be Capital Grille. Capital Grille does not aspire to be CAVA.

The ecosystem is self-sorting, and dare I say self-deporting in cases of a mismatch.

Wawa appears here as a minor but telling data point. 1,282 transactions across 320 committees. This is a campaign that eats at a gas station convenience store. These are the people you’ll want to hire one day.

This tier includes Hooters, which has dozens of visits enshrined permanently in the FEC, along with the hit it took during Covid.

Peak During Pumpkin Spice Season

50%+ of transactions occur in the final 60 days.

During campaign season, no one is charging Landini Brothers on King Street in Alexandria to a campaign committee. The Hamilton, the Longworth House Office Building cafeteria, Oceanaire, even Schneider’s of Capitol Hill all do 85% of their campaign-expensed business at other times. But a separate group sits far above that line: staff and volunteers where the majority of activity happens in the final stretch. Each row has its own reason for being here:

The Safest Place in Washington

More interesting than the partisan splits is the list of restaurants that show no meaningful split at all. These are the shared rooms. The places where people who treat each other like enemies in public are operating in the same environments in private.

The same consultants who won’t share data will both order the cheese board at Sonoma.

Door Dash and Uber Eats Everything Else.

The rise of delivery platforms in the FEC record is its own sub-ecosystem, and it leans Democratic by both spend and transaction count across all major platforms. My theory mirrors what we observed with Domino’s: the winner in each category is the platform with the least friction in its ordering interface. Campaigns, operating at high cognitive load, route toward minimum resistance. The platform that removes friction captures the system. Everything else is preference.

Curiosa

There are a handful of places where the parties don’t just overlap, they dissolve. The data surfaces a small class of restaurants with near-perfect partisan entropy, and at the center of that map sits Thunder Grill, nestled right in the bosom of transience.

A Tex-Mex chain restaurant tucked by the front door of Union Station, it borrowed liberally from the most mediocre pieces of everyone else’s menu. Barely earned its 2.8 stars on Yelp. Nothing about it was distinctive, and that was precisely the point. Campaigns from both parties spent there in almost equal measure, not because they agreed, but because the space stripped them of identity. It was the kind of place you would walk by hundreds of times and wonder what it tastes like. I can assure you, Thunder Grill did not deserve to live.

Also, this piece wasn’t about restaurants.
