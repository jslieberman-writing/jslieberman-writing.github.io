The Leftovers

Jordan Lieberman

Feb 09, 2026

They’re not gone. They’re just not here.

There’s a concept called Invisible String. It promises that people stay connected even when you can’t see the thread between them. I spent a year finding invisible strings in tens of millions of campaign finance transactions to make sense of the world, to find out what makes relationships last when they aren’t under the same roof, the same company name, or even the same state. The strings are real. It’s basically string theory for people who failed physics but have SQL access. If you have campaign experience, you know what I am talking about.

The data shows how political careers actually work. It knows when you left FEC visibility and where you went next. It can predict your next thirty years. It knows why you left and when you left. It doesn’t care about your narrative. It just observes.

All it took was a little computing power, turning 50, and wondering how I got here.

Perfectly Ordinary

Twenty-seven years ago I cold-called what happened to be the organization in America offering one of the most promising campaign manager career paths in politics and public affairs. The New Jersey Republican State Committee has the highest three-year survival rate of any political organization in my dataset. I know because I was one of them.

It wasn’t my pretty smile. Nobody asked where I went to college. Chris Brennan picked up the phone when I called looking for a job. He scribbled “wants job” on a Post-it. He introduced me to Steve Issenman, who introduced me to David Wildstein, who turned out to be among the biggest bridge nodes in New Jersey political history.

Of the four campaign manager archetypes, I was the Nepo manager because the mayor called the congressman and got me an internship in a congressional office in 1995.

Fast-forward a perfectly ordinary number of job changes and career choices, and now I run Powers Interactive.

I barely passed calculus. Cried my way through economics. I got a gentleman’s C in grad school statistics. What I had was a network. The calculus, econ, and stats classes I barely passed didn’t matter. The people who picked up the phone did.

The data doesn’t care about that part. It just knows that moment put me on a track with a predictable probability of ending up exactly where I am. I thought I was navigating a tricky career path. Like you, I was being sorted.

The bar at the Pollies does not care what party you work for.

I survived in professional politics because I found a series of bridge nodes. A bridge connects networks. It doesn’t control the system, but it sees a lot. Some people call them connectors. Between PoliticsNJ, PoliticsPA, and Campaigns & Elections, I had about ten years of being the connector by profession. Political consultants from every part of the spectrum would tell me their private thoughts.

A little secret: you’re all the same. Drunk, tired, and in private, Bridge nodes get to see the private version of you. Your marriages, resentments, addictions, humiliations, regrets. You ask for introductions, advice, and absolution. The network doesn’t just route work through bridge nodes. It routes everyone’s leaky bag of poo. Everyone carries a leaky bag of poo through life; some are leakier, but all stink.

That’s the secret life of network centrality nobody tells you. You are a bus station whether you are crumbling on the inside or not. From where I sat, the patterns were unmistakable: a career that punishes work-life balance, leaves relationships strained, and only recently started talking honestly about mental health. Despite that, you’re still here.

It took nearly thirty years to live this, but just a week to see it in some Python code. I thought leaving campaign payroll meant I was done with all of you. Wrong again.

This is the arm of my friend Rory McShane. Every state he worked in is permanently marked. Some other friends added Bernie ink to their feet. I got grey hair instead. You’re all the same.

Ambassador Clown and PoliticsPA

Let me rewind to the moment I knew the shit-eating campaign phase was over.

It was 2001, on a statewide in New York. The candidate considered everything above 96th Street upstate New York. He mostly hid in his basement in the Hamptons after 9/11. Naturally, he later became ambassador to a large Central European country.

Previous campaigns I’d heartily believed in the candidates. This time I was serving an absolute clown. Our likely opponent happened to be my grad school professor. It was a weird time. But 9/11 changed the primary math. I didn’t quit. Mercifully, the candidate did.

The timing worked out fine. I’d been involved with the business operation of PoliticsNJ, which launched six months earlier. (On October 23, 2001, I quietly created PoliticsPA and wrote it anonymously as “Sy Snyder”) until selling it five years later. Eventually, an investor approached wanting to start something at the federal level. I suggested he buy Campaigns & Elections magazine. He did, and made me the publisher in 2005.

At 29, it was a dream job. Turns out that campaign-adjacent was as close as I would go. The data says that losing three consecutive races in multiple states is exactly the right history to maximize networks and conclude it’s time to get on the bus and head to the next destination.

First prize is a Cadillac Eldorado. Second prize is a set of steak knives. Third prize is you’re fired.

The magazine owner was an unkind, unfair bully. We never came close to breaking even. He played Alec Baldwin in Glengarry Glen Ross in his own head, barking orders and humiliating staff.

There are two teenage girls alive today who don’t know their mom worked on the Political Pages Directory from the maternity ward. The owner had me fire my best friend. On a holiday. He asked me to write a false column about Hillary Clinton’s sexual preference. I refused. He never forgave me. He had me contribute to his favorite political candidates. In 2010 I stopped fighting and quit working at a magazine I had adored since junior high.

I fled the company and kept the network. According to my research, that’s the optimal path for anyone in professional politics.

Nothing else held.

The decade that followed was not kind. The network held. Nothing else did.

After 23 years in DC, I moved to South Carolina. The town has two traffic lights and a couple hundred alligators. Turns out the invisible strings are more meaningful and tangible from a distance, because that’s what is left. The music gets softer but more understandable.

It wasn’t brilliance or courage. I followed a very common path and mistook it for agency. I didn’t know I was on the conveyor belt. Having quantified thousands of campaign manager resumes and what they do after, I followed the same pattern. Job change velocity in our business drops by half after each promotion. Like uranium, our mobility has a half-life. Promotions don’t just raise you, they trap you.

The others? They’re not gone. They’re just not here.

The data tracks who disappears from FEC visibility. Our lives track who disappears from the frame. The Leftovers isn’t about the ones who vanished, it’s about the people left behind trying to make sense of the absence. This project isn’t really about the conveyor belt. It’s about looking at the data and finally being able to see the shape of what’s missing.

The data doesn’t care that Chris Brennan scribbled “wants job” on a Post-it. It doesn’t care about the invisible strings that connect you to people who aren’t here anymore. It just observes the pattern. It just shows you who’s left over. We can’t see them, but we know what shaped their life. And that makes all the difference.

This project started out of curiosity about how campaigns sort people. It became something else: an attempt to understand what makes some connections survive and others break. The data can map networks, but it can’t tell you how to maintain the ones that matter most. That part you have to figure out yourself.

And here’s the least surprising part of the story: I met my wife at the Pollies. At a party attended by both Republicans and Democrats of all kinds of tribes. The invisible strings that run through tens of millions of transactions ran right through the open bar and Chris Brennan read from the Book of Springsteen at my wedding, 10 years ago last month.
