The Waiting Place

Jordan Lieberman

Nov 04, 2026

The American Campaign Operative, Part IV

In 1999, I slept on a couch above a dental office. I was 23 years old, making $2,000 a month working for the two worst candidates for the New Jersey Assembly. I was eating shit. It was Middlesex County, New Jersey’s worst county.

My friends were getting respectable jobs. They had apartments and employers who intended to remain in business longer than two years. I was told the indignities of a state legislative campaign were the dues I had agreed to pay on the way to bigger things.

I mention this because Election Day is behind you, and I suspect half the people reading this feel roughly the way I did. Your friends from college have 401(k)s. You have a campaign t-shirt. The adrenaline stops cold turkey. And you’re beginning to wonder whether you’ve made a terrible mistake. I sure did.

WHEN SEUSS GOES DARK

There are three copies of Oh, the Places You’ll Go! in my house. They were all gifts to me and my children. It’s a peculiar book to give a young person. Most of it is a cheerful promise about mountains to climb and wonderful things to accomplish.

Then Seuss makes a turn and introduces the Waiting Place. The Waiting Place means nothing when you graduate 5th grade. But when you first visit the place as an adult, it hits different.

It’s where people wait for something to happen, or somebody to tell them what comes next. Seuss doesn’t think much of the place. In politics, The Waiting Place even has a date on the calendar. We call it November.

EVERYBODY HAS A PLAN UNTIL THEY GET PUNCHED IN THE MOUTH

One of the crueler features of being young is the belief that everybody else knows what they’re doing. They don’t.

At 23, what I couldn’t see were their future layoffs, career changes, burnout, failed businesses, or lack of passion for what they do. I mistook a snapshot in time for a forecast. Anxiety is a feature of early adulthood, not a bug. Your current lack of a plan is not evidence that your career is over.

Losing doesn’t hurt your odds of getting hired again. I’ve been studying more than twenty years of federal campaign payrolls. Among roughly 32,000 staffers in the two-year comparison, 10.4% of those on losing campaigns appeared on another federal committee’s payroll within two years. Among those on winning campaigns, 9.9% did. I followed the comparison as far as six years. Losing an election was not, at least by this measure, a career ender. (One caveat on that 10%: federal payroll doesn’t see state campaigns, government jobs, or consulting firms.)

Among campaign workers who started in the bottom quarter of pay and later appeared in another federal payroll episode, 27.9% eventually reached the top quarter. That’s only the people we saw again on federal payroll, not everybody who started at the bottom. Still, it’s a useful GFY to the idea that you are defined by your first paycheck after graduation.

THE DOOR ISN’T WIDE OPEN

None of this means the job market is easy. My numbers do suggest that the verdict you reach about yourself after one miserable week is premature.

In 2012, nine of every ten people on a federal campaign payroll in the final month were rookies. By 2024 it was closer to eight in ten. More of the jobs are going to people who’ve had them before, because the ladder is not what it used to be. At the lower end, real pay fell roughly 35% over the period I studied. It’s jarring when your potential employer ghosts you harder than your ex from college, but they don’t tell you this at graduation.

It’s tempting for someone my age to tell you to tough it out because we all had it hard. That would be lazy. Some things really are harder. Your anxiety is real. This period of your life is also a crappy career forecaster.

MAKE YOUR OWN LUCK

I heard the smartest job advice. It was to get a job on a winning presidential campaign. Sometimes it’s just dumb luck, and sometimes you have to make your own luck. It’s a little cruel, because sometimes there really aren’t any jobs. Campaign operatives know how to work hard. That’s rarely the problem.

Careers are seldom delivered as neatly packaged opportunities. You make yourself useful. You meet people. You take the job that sounds unimpressive. You move somewhere you hadn’t intended to live. Sometimes you work outside politics for a while. My best hires have not followed the traditional path. Sometimes you discover that you don’t actually want to come back. That’s a perfectly respectable outcome, too.

I know how these stories get told on LinkedIn. Every detour becomes a brilliant strategic decision. Every humiliating job becomes an important apprenticeship. Every unspeakable act of loyalty becomes a career pivot. It’s a nice story, but largely fiction. In 1999, I wasn’t building a personal brand. I was sleeping above a dental office and eating shit in Middlesex County, the home to fourteen active Superfund sites.

Someday someone in my family will read about The Waiting Place at exactly the moment they enter it. I hope they enjoy the mountains and the adventures. I also hope they don’t mistake the Waiting Place for proof that they’ve failed. The life you’re living right now is not the final verdict on the life you’re going to have. You’re allowed to spend a little time in the Waiting Place.

You’re allowed to sit down in the Waiting Place, friend.

Just know that the Waiting Place isn’t the end.
