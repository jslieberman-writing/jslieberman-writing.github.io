Your Family Portrait

Jordan Lieberman

Mar 17, 2026

Family portraits are always awkward. They capture not just the family, but the context of time, space, and culture.

Twenty years ago, this map of political and public affairs consultant shops was fancy enough to post on social media.

Fast forward, eight years, this time inspired by the LUMAscape. The result was wrong. (Not a little wrong.) I thought the political professional industry could be mapped the way Silicon Valley maps itself: as a marketplace of products. It can’t. This business is not organized around software. It is organized around relationships. What you see below is a logo farm intended to emulate the LUMAscape. The map was intended to show the political LUMAscape, a nonpartisan taxonomy of tools, vendors, and categories that reflected how the industry thought it worked.

Higher Ground Labs later published their own version of the same idea, updating the categories and logos for a new cycle, focusing on Democrats. Different author, same underlying incorrect assumption: that this ecosystem could be understood as a marketplace of products rather than a network of people.

You can’t understand our ecosystem without understanding the labor market. This is the key point that tech vendors of all sizes have misunderstood for the last two decades. The talent flow from politics to tech is healthy. The reverse is basically nonexistent. Nobody is quitting Google to run Norm Van Houten for Assembly.

Who hired whom, who married who, and who disappeared into “consulting” all matter. Network theory is the discipline that studies how those connections form and how money, information, and blame flow through them. It was developed to model power grids and disease outbreaks, which is a perfect analogy for all of you.

The key insight will offend you. Your position in the network matters more than how good you are. Your deck helps. Your case studies don’t. You still have to close the deal, but your eligibility to win it is determined by where you sit in the network.

In this data, mobility drops sharply after roughly a decade in the industry; once consultants spend seven to ten years inside a professional network, you rarely switch tribes. You are married.

In the last twenty years, there have been 2.6M vendors at the state level and 1.2M vendors at the federal level. About 1% of each group bill more than $250k, which is the approximate threshold where company survival rates start looking better. That also qualifies you to be in the chart you are about to see.

Your Family Portrait

Every family has a family portrait. Most leave question marks. Should we include the girlfriend? Who is missing?

Here is your family portrait. It contains more than 69,000 organizations connected by shared campaigns, firms, and institutional history. It’s measurable all the same.

The most valuable people understand the pipes, how a political ad gets bought, and how to leverage this information. Each connection reflects shared work, shared firms, shared campaigns, or shared institutional history. The density is not accidental. The clustering is not random. This is decades of accumulated relationships rendered as a living system. This is the Lumascape with more accurate lines, but still missing the tribal human element.

Relationships do not disappear. They can’t. The invisible string is forever. They migrate almost always within tribes. If you attend the Pollies, C&E events, Public Affairs Council, BISC, CPAC or other similar events, your organization is most certainly in this image, color coded by tribe; the familiar people you drink with are most likely in the same tribe as you. Your language is the same, your passions are similar, your shared traumas are relatable.

There are not two parties at this level. There are about ten large tribes and dozens of smaller ones, all connected internally through shared traumas, clients, and staff.

Therein lies the challenge: every bit of research on career success in this business depends on your ability to transcend the expected invisible strings and make your own. The best thing you can do for your career is create unexpected lines in this chart.
